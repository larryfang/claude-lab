# Your Next 30 Days

You have the skills. Whether they become a habit depends almost entirely on the next four weeks.

## The honest risk

The usual outcome after a course like this is a burst of enthusiasm, three impressive one-off runs, and then a slow return to doing things the old way — because the old way is a habit and this is not yet.

The fix is not motivation. It is picking one recurring thing and defending it.

## Week 1 — Ship one real thing

- [ ] Run your capstone machine for real, on real data
- [ ] Verify it with the four checks
- [ ] **Send it to someone.** A manager, a stakeholder, a customer-facing colleague
- [ ] Write down how long it took, including verification

The sending matters. Output nobody receives is practice, not work, and practice does not create the accountability that makes a habit stick.

## Week 2 — Make it boring

- [ ] Run the same thing again. It should be faster and less interesting
- [ ] Fix the one thing that annoyed you last week — in the Skill, not by hand
- [ ] Add your second play: pick the next-most-annoying recurring task
- [ ] Read your scheduled job's output properly. Trace important numbers and sample additional ones

Boring is the goal. The first run is exciting; the fourth run is a capability.

## Week 3 — Bring one person

- [ ] Give one colleague your Skill, Project instructions, and three example requests
- [ ] Let them run it unsupervised
- [ ] Fix what confused them
- [ ] Ask what they would want next

## Week 4 — Consolidate

- [ ] Review your Skills. Delete any you have not used
- [ ] Review your scheduled jobs. Delete any whose last three outputs you did not read
- [ ] Ask your Project what it takes as established context, and correct it
- [ ] Update your `ACCESS-LOG.md`
- [ ] Write down, honestly, what this has actually changed about your week

Then put a quarterly reminder in your calendar for that review. Twenty minutes, four times a year, and it is what stops the whole setup rotting.

## The five habits that separate people who keep this

1. **Brief, do not prompt.** When output disappoints, ask which B.R.I.E.F. letter was missing. Fix the brief, not the document.
2. **Always ask for two artefacts.** Data and narrative. Without the data you cannot verify the narrative.
3. **Verify according to the consequences.** Check decision-critical claims, reconcile the data, and sample beyond the headline. Three numbers is a practice sample, not a release criterion.
4. **Skill it after the fourth run.** Not the first — you do not yet know what good looks like.
5. **Delete what you do not use.** Unread scheduled output and unused Skills are worse than nothing; they create false confidence about how much is automated.

Keep learning from your own work too:

- **Read your own outputs critically.** Your best teacher is the error you find in your own deliverable at 9pm before a review.
- **Talk to whoever else at your company is doing this.** The most useful thing you will exchange is not prompts; it is the definitions and reference files that encode how your company actually works.
- **The other lanes in this course.** If you did Sales, the GTM lane will tell you why marketing writes what it writes. If you are a PM, the Sales lane will change how you write release notes.

## The one thing to remember

:::concept The whole course in three sentences
Describe the outcome, not the steps. Name the sources, set the rules, and ask to be told what you should decide rather than having it decided for you. Then verify before you trust — because output that looks finished and output that is correct are different things, and only one of them protects you.
:::

Everything else — Skills, schedules, Projects, connectors — is machinery for doing that repeatedly and at scale.
